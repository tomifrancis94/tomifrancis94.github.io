3 EDT and CDT

3.1 Evidential decision theory: formulation 1#

Read the requirement as probabilistic independence. Then each state has one credence, whichever act we are evaluating.

Principle ((Evidential) expected utility)
\mathrm{EU}_e(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Cr(S_i)\,U(A, S_i)

Both theories accept this equation. They disagree about how the states are to be construed. This is evidential decision theory only if the states are probabilistically independent of your actions.

3.2 Evidential decision theory: formulation 2#

With a second formula, we don’t actually need the states to be probabilistically independent of the acts.

Principle (Evidential expected utility, generalised)
\mathrm{EU}_e(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Cr(S_i \mid A)\,U(A, S_i)

All we now require is that each state, with each act, settles everything you care about. And where the states are probabilistically independent of the acts, \Cr(S_i \mid A) = \Cr(S_i) — so the two agree. Notice that dominance reasoning does not apply for partitions that are not probabilistically independent of the acts.

3.3 Newcomb, on the evidential theory#

Taking the demon to be right 95\% of the time:

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{EU}_e(\text{one box}) &= 1{,}000{,}000(0.95) + 0(0.05) &&= 950{,}000\\[0.3em] \mathrm{EU}_e(\text{both}) &= 1{,}001{,}000(0.05) + 1{,}000(0.95) &&= 51{,}000 \end{aligned}

These use the second formulation. The prediction states are not probabilistically independent of the acts, so the credences here are the ones conditional on the act: given that you take one box, the demon predicted one box with probability 0.95; given that you take both, it predicted both with probability 0.95.

So evidential decision theory says take one box.

3.4 Causal decision theory#

Take the other reading: the states must be causally independent of what you do. All the work now goes into saying which states those are.

Definition (Dependency hypothesis (Lewis))

A maximally specific proposition about how the things you care about do and do not depend causally on your present actions.

3.5 What one actually looks like#

Take one counterfactual for each option: if I were to do A, the outcome would be O, written A \;\square\!\rightarrow\; O. A dependency hypothesis is a whole pattern of these — one per option, all at once.

Lewis’s example. Three options, and Bruce the cat might purr loudly, softly, or not at all.

I brush Bruce \square\!\rightarrow he purrs loudly
I stroke Bruce \square\!\rightarrow he purrs softly
I leave Bruce alone \square\!\rightarrow he doesn’t purr
I brush Bruce \square\!\rightarrow he doesn’t purr
I stroke Bruce \square\!\rightarrow he doesn’t purr
I leave Bruce alone \square\!\rightarrow he doesn’t purr

You have no influence over which pattern holds, so each pattern is causally act-independent by construction.

3.6 Newcomb’s dependency hypotheses#
K_1:one box \square\!\rightarrow \$1{,}000{,}000
both boxes \square\!\rightarrow \$1{,}001{,}000
K_2:one box \square\!\rightarrow \$0
both boxes \square\!\rightarrow \$1{,}000

Neither of these says what you will choose — only what your choosing affects.

These are not the only dependency hypotheses there are, but they are the only ones an agent should entertain in this scenario.

3.7 Utility#
Principle (Causal expected utility)
\mathrm{EU}_c(A) = \sum_K \Cr(K)\,U(A, K)

Note that the credences are unconditional. Your act might be evidence about which K holds — just as it was about the prediction. Conditioning the credence in K on act A would let that evidence back in.

Applied to Newcomb’s problem, write k for \Cr(K_1), so that \Cr(K_2) = 1 - k:

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{EU}_c(\text{one box}) &= 1{,}000{,}000\,k + 0\,(1-k) &&= 1{,}000{,}000\,k\\[0.3em] \mathrm{EU}_c(\text{both}) &= 1{,}001{,}000\,k + 1{,}000\,(1-k) &&= 1{,}000{,}000\,k + 1{,}000 \end{aligned}

Whatever k is, taking both boxes comes out \$1{,}000 ahead. So causal decision theory says take both boxes.
