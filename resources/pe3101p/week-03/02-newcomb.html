2 Newcomb's problem

2.1 Two boxes#

A transparent box, holding \$1{,}000. You can see that it does. A blue box, holding either \$1{,}000{,}000 or nothing. You may take the blue box alone, or you may take both.

2.2 Newcomb’s problem (with added demons)#

Last night, while you slept, a demon scanned your brain and predicted what you would do in Two Boxes. If the demon predicted you would take one box, they put \$1{,}000{,}000 in the blue box. If the demon predicted you would take both boxes, they left the blue box empty. The demon has done this many times, and has almost always been right.

2.3 The case for two-boxing#

The money is already in the blue box, or it’s already not there. Nothing you do now changes it. So — take the extra money!

2.4 The case for one-boxing#

People who take one box almost always end up millionaires. People who take both boxes almost always end up with only one thousand dollars. If you take only one box, you are way more likely to be one of the millionaires. So — take one box and enjoy being rich!

2.5 The obvious table#

Taking the states of nature to be what the demon predicted last night:

Predicted one boxPredicted both
Take the blue box\$1{,}000{,}000\$0
Take both boxes\$1{,}001{,}000\$1{,}000

Taking both gets you \$1{,}000 more in either column. So taking both boxes strongly statewise dominates taking only the blue box.

2.6 Are these the right states of nature?#

Recall the conditions on states of nature from week 1:

  • (i) Together with the act, they resolve the uncertainty and guarantee one outcome.
  • (ii) The act you choose does not affect whether the state occurs, or how probable it is.
  • (iii) They are mutually exclusive.
  • (iv) They are jointly exhaustive.
2.7 Should states of nature satisfy probabilistic independence?#
Conditions (i), (iii) and (iv) are all fine. But your choice is strong evidence about what the demon predicted. Write B for taking both boxes, and D for the demon predicting that you would take both boxes:
\Cr(D \mid B) \approx 1 , \qquad \Cr(D \mid B^c) \approx 0 .

By the law of total probability, \Cr(D) = \Cr(B)\Cr(D \mid B) + \Cr(B^c)\Cr(D \mid B^c), which with those two values is approximately \Cr(B). So \Cr(D \mid B) \approx 1 while \Cr(D) \approx \Cr(B), and the two come apart unless you are already all but certain that you will take both boxes. So \Cr(D \mid B) \neq \Cr(D). Which is exactly the failure of probabilistic independence.

2.8 What that shows, and what it doesn’t#

It does not prove the states are wrong. It shows there’s a dispute about what the relevant states should be. Condition (ii) can be read as requiring two things that are incompatible:

  • (a) Probabilistic independence.
  • (b) Causal independence.
2.9 Probabilistic and causal independence#
Definition (Probabilistic independence)
The set of states of nature is probabilistically independent of the acts if and only if, for every state S_i and every act A,
\Cr(S_i \mid A) = \Cr(S_i).
Definition (Causal independence)

The set of states of nature is causally independent of the acts if and only if which action we take makes no difference to the probabilities of the states.

This second notion isn’t as clearly defined as the first. But it is intuitive.

2.10 Why they came apart#

A common cause, and no arrow from your choice to the prediction. Which is why the correlation isn’t an influence.

2.11 Two state conditions, two theories#
  • Require probabilistic independence of the acts \rightarrow evidential decision theory.
  • Require causal independence of the acts \rightarrow causal decision theory.
