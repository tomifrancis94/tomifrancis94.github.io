7 The Expected Utility Theorem

Theorem 1 (Expected Utility Theorem)

Let \succsim be a preference relation over prospects.

  • (i) \succsim satisfies the four axioms if and only if some function u on outcomes represents it by expected utility: for all prospects P and Q,
    P \succsim Q \quad\text{iff}\quad \EU(P) \geq \EU(Q), \qquad\text{where } \EU(P) = \sum_{x} P(x)\,u(x),
    the sum running over the finitely many outcomes P gives positive probability to.
  • (ii) Any two such functions u and u' differ only in unit and zero: u' = a \cdot u + c for some a > 0 and some c.
7.1 The idea of the proof#

Pick two prospects, one better than the other: A_1 \succ A_0. The choice is more or less arbitrary. Assign u(A_1) = 1 and u(A_0) = 0: the unit and the zero. For any B between them, there is exactly one p with B \sim \mix{A_1}{p}{A_0}. Assign u(B) = p.

If B \succ A_1, there is exactly one p with A_1 \sim \mix{B}{p}{A_0}; assign u(B) = \nicefrac{1}{p}. If A_0 \succ B, there is exactly one p with A_0 \sim \mix{A_1}{p}{B}; assign u(B) = -\nicefrac{p}{(1-p)}.

The rest of the proof fills in the details, and shows that this assignment represents the whole of the agent’s preferences: the ranking by u is the ranking by \succsim, and u respects mixing. The other direction: preferences that violate any one of the axioms cannot be represented by a utility function.

7.2 Beginning the proof#

The proof follows Fishburn, The Foundations of Expected Utility (1982), ch. 2, Theorem 1. The same proof is Theorem 8.4 of his Utility Theory for Decision Making (1970).

If no prospect is strictly preferred to any other, set u constant. Done. Otherwise fix two prospects A_1 \succ A_0: the unit and the zero.

7.3 Step 1: more of the better thing is better#
Lemma 1 (more of the better thing is better)

If A \succ B and p, q \in [0,1], then \mix{A}{p}{B} \succ \mix{A}{q}{B} iff p > q.

1 / 3
Proof of Lemma 1 (more of the better thing is better)

Given A \succ B and 0 < q < p < 1 (the edge cases q = 0 and p = 1 take one step each). Write s = \nicefrac{(p-q)}{(1-q)}, so that s + (1-s)q = p.

\begin{aligned} A &\succ B && \text{the hypothesis} \\ \mix{A}{q}{A} &\succ \mix{A}{q}{B} && \text{Independence, mixing $A$ in front of both sides at weight $q$} \\ A &\succ \mix{A}{q}{B} && \text{(M1) on the left} \\ \mix{A}{s}{(\mix{A}{q}{B})} &\succ \mix{(\mix{A}{q}{B})}{s}{(\mix{A}{q}{B})} && \text{Independence again, at weight $s$} \\ \mix{A}{s}{(\mix{A}{q}{B})} &\succ \mix{A}{q}{B} && \text{(M1) on the right} \\ \mix{A}{[s + (1-s)q]}{B} &\succ \mix{A}{q}{B} && \text{(M4) on the left, with $s$ in the role of $p$} \\ \mix{A}{p}{B} &\succ \mix{A}{q}{B}. && \text{$s + (1-s)q = p$, by the choice of $s$} \end{aligned}

That is one direction. For the other: if p = q the two sides are the same prospect, and if p < q the argument just given puts \mix{A}{q}{B} strictly above \mix{A}{p}{B}. Either way, not \mix{A}{p}{B} \succ \mix{A}{q}{B}.

∎
7.4 Step 2: a mixture of worse (or equal) prospects is worse (or equal)#
Lemma 2 (a mixture of worse (or equal) prospects is worse (or equal))

If A \succsim X and A \succsim Y, then A \succsim \mix{X}{p}{Y}.

Proof
\begin{aligned} A &\succsim X && \text{the first hypothesis} \\ \mix{A}{p}{Y} &\succsim \mix{X}{p}{Y} && \text{Independence, mixing both sides with $Y$ at weight $p$} \\ A &\succsim Y && \text{the second hypothesis} \\ \mix{A}{p}{A} &\succsim \mix{A}{p}{Y} && \text{Independence, mixing $A$ in front of both sides} \\ A &\succsim \mix{A}{p}{Y} && \text{(M1) on the left} \\ A &\succsim \mix{A}{p}{Y} \succsim \mix{X}{p}{Y} && \text{the two results together} \\ A &\succsim \mix{X}{p}{Y}. && \text{transitivity} \end{aligned}

A mixture of prospects that A is at least as good as is itself one that A is at least as good as. The same proof with the preferences reversed gives the mirror image.

∎
Definition (Interval)

For prospects A \succsim B, the interval [B, A] is the set of prospects X with A \succsim X \succsim B.

By Lemma 2 (a mixture of worse (or equal) prospects is worse (or equal)) and its mirror image, an interval is closed under mixing: if X and Y are in [B, A], so is \mix{X}{q}{Y}. Any finitely many prospects have a best and a worst among them, by Completeness and Transitivity. So they all lie in one interval.

7.5 Step 3: calibration on an interval#
Lemma 3 (calibration on an interval)

Fix A \succ B. For every X in [B, A] there is exactly one p \in [0,1] with X \sim \mix{A}{p}{B}.

Proof

Existence. If X \sim A, take p = 1; if X \sim B, take p = 0. Otherwise A \succ X \succ B, and Continuity supplies a p.

Uniqueness. Two different values p > q would give \mix{A}{p}{B} \succ \mix{A}{q}{B} by Lemma 1 (more of the better thing is better), but both are \sim X: a contradiction, by transitivity.

∎
Definition

For X in [B, A], u_{AB}(X) is the unique p with X \sim \mix{A}{p}{B}.

There is one calibration per interval; the subscript keeps track of which ends were used. u_{AB}(A) = 1 and u_{AB}(B) = 0, by (M1).

7.6 Step 4: u_{AB} represents \succsim on the interval#
Lemma 4 (u_{AB} represents \succsim on the interval)

For X and Y in [B, A]: X \succsim Y iff u_{AB}(X) \geq u_{AB}(Y).

Proof

Write p = u_{AB}(X) and q = u_{AB}(Y), so that X \sim \mix{A}{p}{B} and Y \sim \mix{A}{q}{B}.

\begin{aligned} X \succsim Y \quad &\text{iff} \quad \mix{A}{p}{B} \succsim \mix{A}{q}{B} && \text{transitivity, in both directions} \\ &\text{iff} \quad \text{not } \mix{A}{q}{B} \succ \mix{A}{p}{B} && \text{Completeness} \\ &\text{iff} \quad \text{not } q > p && \text{Lemma 1 (more of the better thing is better), in both directions} \\ &\text{iff} \quad p \geq q, \end{aligned}

which is u_{AB}(X) \geq u_{AB}(Y). Every step was an iff.

∎
7.7 Step 5: u_{AB} respects mixing on the interval#
Lemma 5 (u_{AB} respects mixing on the interval)

For X and Y in [B, A] and p \in [0,1]: u_{AB}(\mix{X}{p}{Y}) = p \cdot u_{AB}(X) + (1-p) \cdot u_{AB}(Y).

Proof

The mixture \mix{X}{p}{Y} is in [B, A] by Lemma 2 (a mixture of worse (or equal) prospects is worse (or equal)), so u_{AB} is defined on it. Write q = u_{AB}(X) and r = u_{AB}(Y), so that X \sim \mix{A}{q}{B} and Y \sim \mix{A}{r}{B}.

\begin{aligned} \mix{X}{p}{Y} &\sim \mix{(\mix{A}{q}{B})}{p}{Y} && \text{Independence, mixing $Y$ behind both at weight $p$} \\ &\sim \mix{(\mix{A}{q}{B})}{p}{(\mix{A}{r}{B})} && \text{Independence, on the other component} \\ &= \mix{A}{[pq + (1-p)r]}{B} && \text{arithmetic: the weights are averaged} \end{aligned}

So u_{AB}(\mix{X}{p}{Y}) = pq + (1-p)r by Lemma 3 (calibration on an interval): that is the unique calibrating value.

∎
1 / 2
7.8 Rescaling keeps both properties#
Proposition 4 (rescaling keeps both properties)

Suppose g represents \succsim and respects mixing on some interval. Take a > 0 and any c, and put h = a \cdot g + c. Then h represents \succsim and respects mixing on that interval.

Proof

h represents \succsim. Since a > 0: h(X) \geq h(Y) iff a\,g(X) + c \geq a\,g(Y) + c iff g(X) \geq g(Y) iff X \succsim Y.

h respects mixing. Using c = p\,c + (1-p)\,c:

\begin{aligned} h(\mix{X}{p}{Y}) &= a\,[p\,g(X) + (1-p)\,g(Y)] + c \\ &= p\,[a\,g(X) + c] + (1-p)\,[a\,g(Y) + c] \\ &= p\,h(X) + (1-p)\,h(Y). \end{aligned}
∎

So a rescaling with a positive factor loses nothing. We use this twice: next, and for uniqueness.

7.9 The five lemmas#
  • Lemma 1 (more of the better thing is better): if A \succ B and p, q \in [0,1], then \mix{A}{p}{B} \succ \mix{A}{q}{B} iff p > q.
  • Lemma 2 (a mixture of worse (or equal) prospects is worse (or equal)): if A \succsim X and A \succsim Y, then A \succsim \mix{X}{p}{Y}; likewise with \succsim reversed. So an interval is closed under mixing.
  • Lemma 3 (calibration on an interval): fix A \succ B. For every X in [B, A] there is exactly one p \in [0,1] with X \sim \mix{A}{p}{B}. That p is u_{AB}(X).
  • Lemma 4 (u_{AB} represents \succsim on the interval): for X and Y in [B, A], X \succsim Y iff u_{AB}(X) \geq u_{AB}(Y).
  • Lemma 5 (u_{AB} respects mixing on the interval): for X and Y in [B, A], u_{AB}(\mix{X}{p}{Y}) = p \cdot u_{AB}(X) + (1-p) \cdot u_{AB}(Y).
7.10 Step 6: beyond the interval#

Take any prospect Z. Let A be the best of Z, A_1, A_0 and B the worst, so [B, A] contains all three. Calibrate on that interval, then rescale so that A_1 scores 1 and A_0 scores 0:

Definition
f(X) = \frac{u_{AB}(X) - u_{AB}(A_0)}{u_{AB}(A_1) - u_{AB}(A_0)} \qquad \text{for } X \text{ in } [B, A].

The denominator is positive, since A_1 \succ A_0 and u_{AB} represents \succsim. f is a rescaling of u_{AB} with a positive factor, so it still represents \succsim and respects mixing on [B, A]: Lemma 4 (u_{AB} represents \succsim on the interval) and Lemma 5 (u_{AB} respects mixing on the interval), with Proposition 4 (rescaling keeps both properties). Now three cases show what f(Z) must be — without reference to the interval.

1 / 3

In Case 1, f(Z) = \beta \cdot 1 + (1 - \beta) \cdot 0 = \beta. In Case 2, 1 = f(A_1) = \gamma\,f(Z) + (1 - \gamma) \cdot 0, so f(Z) = \nicefrac{1}{\gamma}; and \gamma > 0, since A_1 \succ A_0. In Case 3, 0 = f(A_0) = \alpha \cdot 1 + (1 - \alpha)\,f(Z), so f(Z) = -\nicefrac{\alpha}{(1 - \alpha)}; and \alpha < 1, likewise.

In each case, f(Z) is fixed by an indifference that mentions only Z, A_1 and A_0. The interval played no part. So two intervals containing Z, A_1, A_0 give the same f(Z). Define u(Z) to be that common value. This is exactly the assignment from the idea of the proof: \beta in between, \nicefrac{1}{\gamma} above, -\nicefrac{\alpha}{(1-\alpha)} below.

u is now defined on every prospect. Take any X and Y, and an interval [B, A] containing X, Y, A_1, A_0. On that interval u is the rescaled calibration f, which represents \succsim and respects mixing there. So X \succsim Y iff u(X) \geq u(Y), and u(\mix{X}{q}{Y}) = q\,u(X) + (1-q)\,u(Y).

7.11 Step 7: u is expected utility#

A prospect X gives positive probability to finitely many outcomes, say x_1, \dots, x_n, with probabilities p_1, \dots, p_n. It is a mixture of them, taken one at a time: X = \mix{x_1}{p_1}{X'}, where X' is the rest of X, rescaled to sum to 1. And X' is a mixture in the same way. Since u respects mixing, applying this n - 1 times gives

u(X) = p_1\,u(x_1) + p_2\,u(x_2) + \dots + p_n\,u(x_n) = \EU(X).
Since u represents \succsim: X \succsim Y iff \EU(X) \geq \EU(Y). That is the “only if” half of Theorem 1(i).

7.12 Step 8: uniqueness#
Proof of Theorem 1(ii)

Suppose v also represents \succsim and respects mixing. We want a > 0 and c with v = a \cdot u + c.

Which a and c? Only one choice is possible. At A_0: v(A_0) = a \cdot u(A_0) + c = c. At A_1: v(A_1) = a \cdot u(A_1) + c = a + c. So c = v(A_0) and a = v(A_1) - v(A_0).

The candidate. Put w = a \cdot u + c with those values. By construction, w(A_1) = v(A_1) and w(A_0) = v(A_0). And a > 0, since v represents \succsim and A_1 \succ A_0. So w is a positive rescaling of u: by Proposition 4 (rescaling keeps both properties), it represents \succsim and respects mixing.

What is left: show v(Z) = w(Z) for every Z. Suppose not, for some Z. Three cases, each a contradiction. Throughout, v and w both represent \succsim (so indifferent prospects get equal values), both respect mixing, and they agree at A_1 and A_0.

Case 1: A_1 \succsim Z \succsim A_0, so Z \sim \mix{A_1}{\beta}{A_0}. Both respect mixing, and they agree at the two ends:

v(Z) = \beta\,v(A_1) + (1-\beta)\,v(A_0) = \beta\,w(A_1) + (1-\beta)\,w(A_0) = w(Z).

Case 2: Z \succ A_1, so A_1 \sim \mix{Z}{\gamma}{A_0}, with \gamma > 0. Read the indifference through each function, then compare:

\gamma\,v(Z) + (1-\gamma)\,v(A_0) = v(A_1) = w(A_1) = \gamma\,w(Z) + (1-\gamma)\,w(A_0).
Cancel and divide by \gamma > 0: v(Z) = w(Z).

Case 3: A_0 \succ Z, so A_0 \sim \mix{A_1}{\alpha}{Z}, with \alpha < 1. The same, at the other end:

\alpha\,v(A_1) + (1-\alpha)\,v(Z) = v(A_0) = w(A_0) = \alpha\,w(A_1) + (1-\alpha)\,w(Z).
Cancel and divide by 1 - \alpha > 0: v(Z) = w(Z).

Contradiction in every case. So v = w = a \cdot u + c, with a = v(A_1) - v(A_0) > 0 and c = v(A_0).

∎
Proposition 5 (rescaled expected utility is expected utility)

For any a > 0 and c, with u' = a \cdot u + c: \EU'(P) = a \cdot \EU(P) + c, so \EU'(P) \geq \EU'(Q) exactly when \EU(P) \geq \EU(Q).

Proof
\begin{aligned} \EU'(P) &= \sum_{x} P(x)\,u'(x) && \text{expected utility computed with $u'$} \\ &= \sum_{x} P(x)\,[a\,u(x) + c] && \text{substitute $u' = a\,u + c$} \\ &= a \sum_{x} P(x)\,u(x) + c \sum_{x} P(x) && \text{collect the $a$ terms and the $c$ terms} \\ &= a \sum_{x} P(x)\,u(x) + c \cdot 1 && \text{the probabilities sum to $1$} \\ &= a \cdot \EU(P) + c. && \text{an increasing linear function of $\EU$} \end{aligned}

Since a > 0, \EU'(P) \geq \EU'(Q) exactly when \EU(P) \geq \EU(Q).

∎
7.13 Step 9: the other direction#

Suppose \succsim is represented by expected utility for some u. Then each axiom holds.

Transitivity and Completeness. \geq on numbers is transitive and complete, and \succsim copies it: P \succsim Q iff \EU(P) \geq \EU(Q).

Independence. \EU(\mix{A}{p}{C}) = p\,\EU(A) + (1-p)\,\EU(C), so

\EU(\mix{A}{p}{C}) \geq \EU(\mix{B}{p}{C}) \quad\text{iff}\quad p\,\EU(A) \geq p\,\EU(B) \quad\text{iff}\quad \EU(A) \geq \EU(B),
since p > 0. The same for \mix{C}{p}{A} and \mix{C}{p}{B}, since 1 - p > 0.

Continuity. Given \EU(A) > \EU(B) > \EU(C), put p = \nicefrac{[\EU(B) - \EU(C)]}{[\EU(A) - \EU(C)]}, which lies in (0,1). Then

\EU(\mix{A}{p}{C}) = \EU(C) + p\,[\EU(A) - \EU(C)] = \EU(B),
so B \sim \mix{A}{p}{C}.

Both directions of (i), and (ii). The theorem is proved.

What we have shown: four axioms on preference hold exactly when some utility function on outcomes represents it — however many outcomes there are. Preference between prospects is exactly comparison of expected utility. The function is fixed once the unit and zero are: an interval scale.
