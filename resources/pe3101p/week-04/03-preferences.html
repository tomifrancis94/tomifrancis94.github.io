3 Preferences

3.1 Notation#

We write A \succsim B when the agent weakly prefers A to B: A is at least as good as B, by her lights. A \succ B is strict preference: A \succsim B and not B \succsim A. A \sim B is indifference: A \succsim B and B \succsim A. So \succsim is the basic notion; the other two are defined from it.

3.2 Preference and choice#

If A \succ B and the choice is between A and B, the agent will choose A, and will not choose B. If B \succ A, the agent will choose B, and will not choose A. If A \sim B, either: choice can’t tell them apart.

We won’t commit to what preference is. Two things matter: it is linked to choice as above, and it is open to rational evaluation. It is defined over every pair of gambles, not just the choices the agent actually faces.

3.4 The ordering principles#
Principle (Reflexivity)

A \succsim A, for every A.

Principle (Transitivity)

If A \succsim B and B \succsim C, then A \succsim C.

Both are among the assumptions of expected utility theory.

?Exercises: the preference order
  1. Show that if A \succ B and B \succ C, then A \succ C.
    Show answer
    From A \succsim B and B \succsim C, transitivity gives A \succsim C. Suppose also that C \succsim A. Then C \succsim A and A \succsim B give C \succsim B by transitivity, which contradicts B \succ C. So not C \succsim A, and hence A \succ C.
  2. Show that if A \sim B and B \succ C, then A \succ C.
    Show answer
    From A \succsim B and B \succsim C, transitivity gives A \succsim C. Suppose also that C \succsim A. Then C \succsim A and A \succsim B give C \succsim B by transitivity, which contradicts B \succ C. So not C \succsim A, and hence A \succ C.
  3. Show that A \succ B and B \succ A cannot both hold.
    Show answer
    A \succ B says, in part, that not B \succsim A. But B \succ A says, in part, that B \succsim A. So they cannot both hold.

Each follows from the definitions plus transitivity. We’ll use facts like these without comment.
