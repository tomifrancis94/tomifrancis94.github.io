5 Prospects

5.1 Prospects#
  • Fix the set of final outcomes. There may be infinitely many.
  • A prospect P assigns a probability P(x) to each outcome x, positive for only finitely many of them; they sum to 1.
  • Finitely many, because a decision problem has finitely many outcomes in play. The preferences themselves range over infinitely many prospects.
Definition (The prospect of a gamble)

The prospect of a gamble G, written P_G, is the function taking each outcome x to the probability that G results in x, computed from your credences in the states.

5.2 From gambles to prospects#
Proposition 3 (from gambles to prospects)

Suppose \succsim satisfies stochastic equivalence and transitivity. If P_G = P_{G'} and P_H = P_{H'}, then G \succsim H iff G' \succsim H'.

Proof

By stochastic equivalence G' \sim G and H \sim H'. Suppose G \succsim H. Then G' \succsim G \succsim H, so G' \succsim H by transitivity; and G' \succsim H \succsim H', so G' \succsim H'. The other direction is the same argument with the primes swapped.

∎
Definition (Preference over prospects)

For prospects P and Q: P \succsim Q iff G \succsim H for some gambles G, H with P_G = P and P_H = Q.

  • Well defined, by Proposition 3 (from gambles to prospects): any gambles with those prospects give the same answer.
  • G \succsim H iff P_G \succsim P_H. The preferences over gambles are completely fixed by the preferences over prospects.
  • Assumed, not proved here: if the ordering properties hold of \succsim over gambles then, given stochastic equivalence, they hold of \succsim over prospects.
  • Also in the background: every prospect is the prospect of some gamble.
5.3 Mixing#
Definition (Mixing)

For prospects A and B and p \in [0,1], \mix{A}{p}{B} is the prospect with

(\mix{A}{p}{B})(x) = p \cdot A(x) + (1-p) \cdot B(x) \qquad \text{for each outcome } x.

  • “A with probability p, otherwise B.”
  • A and B need not be single outcomes. They can themselves be prospects.
1 / 3
  • This is an equation, not a preference. Prospects with the same chances are the same prospect.
5.4 Four facts about mixing#
  • (M1) \mix{A}{p}{A} = A.
  • (M2) \mix{A}{p}{B} = \mix{B}{(1-p)}{A}.
  • (M3) \mix{(\mix{A}{p}{B})}{q}{B} = \mix{A}{(pq)}{B}.
  • (M4) \mix{A}{p}{(\mix{A}{q}{B})} = \mix{A}{[p + (1-p)q]}{B}.
  • All four are arithmetic. Compute the chance of each outcome on both sides.
  • The proof leans on (M1) and (M4).
5.5 The mixture space#
  • The set of all prospects over the outcomes, together with the mixing operation, is a mixture space.
  • From here on, \succsim is a relation over prospects. This is justified by the Stochastic Equivalence assumption.
  • Single outcomes can be identified with prospects too: x with the prospect that gives x for certain.
5.6 What we are going to prove#
  • The first leg is done: \succsim now ranges over prospects.
  • The theorem: \succsim satisfies the axioms if and only if it is represented by expected utility, for some u on outcomes.
  • Represented: P \succsim Q iff \EU(P) \geq \EU(Q), for every pair of prospects. The ranking by \succsim and the ranking by expected utility are the same ranking.
  • Two axioms so far: reflexivity and transitivity. Three to come.
