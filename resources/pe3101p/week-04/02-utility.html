2 Preference and utility

2.1 What utility is#

Many formulas so far have had a U in them. We never said what it is.

Utility as pleasure: an outcome has high utility to the extent that it increases pleasure and reduces pain. Put into MEU: rational agents maximise expected pleasure.

Utility as value: an outcome has utility to the extent that it is actually valuable, whether in the prudential, moral, or another sense. MEU then becomes MEV: Maximise Expected Value.

Utility as degree of desire: how much the agent wants the outcome, all things considered, whatever it is she wants.

Utility as a pure representative device: utility need not have any psychological connection with the agent, and is merely a mathematical representation of preferences the agent already has.

Words can mean what we want them to, but I generally use utility to refer to the representative device. This is the sense we will mostly be concerned with here.

2.2 Correcting some misconceptions (BDRC)#

Misconception 1. BDRC, on the MEP Principle (“rational agents maximize their expected degree of pleasure”):

“The MEP Principle is a form of psychological hedonism. Psychological hedonism is the view that the only thing that ultimately motivates people is their own pleasure and pain.”

False. Psychological hedonism is indeed a thesis about what ultimately motivates people. This makes it independent of the MEP Principle. As stated, the MEP Principle is a thesis about what people should do: what people do insofar as they are rational. The MEP Principle could be false and psychological hedonism true; or the MEP Principle true and psychological hedonism false.

Misconception 2. BDRC:

“The utility of a[n] outcome measures the extent to which the agent in question wants the outcome to obtain.” (BDRC, p. 76)

Schwarz is fixing which sense of “utility” he will use: degree of desire. Fine as a stipulation. But it is not our sense, and it is not what expected utility theory delivers.

If utility represents preferences in the way we will see this week, then utility does align with what the agent wants (prefers), in the sense that outcomes or prospects with higher utility are preferred to ones with lower utility. But that is the only sense in which utility measures what the agent wants. There is no requirement that utility match up with the extent to which an agent wants the outcome or prospect. What utility matches up with is the agent’s attitudes to risk. One outcome having relatively high utility compared to another means that the agent will prefer even relatively small probabilities of the higher-utility outcome. That’s all it means.

2.3 Some more about rationality: what do we mean?#

Rationality, at minimum, seems to demand a kind of coherence between our attitudes, preferences, and actions. If we prefer A to B, and we are offered a choice between A and B, and nothing else is at stake, it seems irrational to choose B. If we believe that it is raining and also believe that it is not raining, that seems irrational too.

Rationality might be more substantive than this. Schwarz suggests one way it might be: if rational egoism were true, so that it were irrational ever to do anything that was worse for us. But this is not the only way. It could be that we’re required to care about certain things, like love or friendship or pleasure or carrot cake. It could be that we’re always rationally required to do the morally right thing (the moral overridingness thesis). It could be that rationality requires us to go off into the woods somewhere to commune with god.

We don’t need to assume that rationality is or is not substantive. The assumptions we’re making about rationality are deliberately quite minimal, and neutral between different visions of what rationality requires — minimal or not.

2.4 What we are going to show#

We start from the agent’s preferences over gambles, not from her utilities. We assume the preferences satisfy some structural principles: the axioms. We then show that there is a utility function u on outcomes such that she prefers one gamble to another exactly when it has higher expected utility, and that u is unique up to unit and zero. This is a representation theorem: the numbers are read off the preferences.
